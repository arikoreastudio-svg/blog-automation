# Gwangjang Market: Seoul's Oldest Food Market, Explained for First-Timers

Gwangjang Market is one of those places every "best of Seoul" list mentions, and it earns the spot. It's loud, crowded, a little chaotic, and full of food you can't easily get anywhere else in the city, all inside one of Korea's oldest continuously running markets.

This guide covers what Gwangjang Market actually is, what to eat there, how ordering and seating actually work, the parts foreigners tend to find confusing, and the practical details (subway, timing, nearby neighborhoods) for a smooth first visit.

## What Is Gwangjang Market?

Gwangjang Market opened in 1905, making it the oldest surviving daily market in Korea. It was founded by a group of Korean merchants who pooled money to create a permanent, privately run market, a genuinely new idea at the time, since most markets before it only opened for a few days at a stretch rather than every day.

Today the market spans a large indoor complex, with thousands of vendors selling everything from silk and hanbok fabric on the upper floor to secondhand clothing and housewares. But most visitors come for one thing: the food section, a dense maze of stalls that's become one of the most famous street food destinations in Seoul.

## Must-Try Foods: A Beginner's Menu

If you don't read Korean and have never seen half of this food before, here's a quick primer on what to look for.

- **Bindaetteok** – a savory, crispy-edged pancake made from ground mung beans, usually mixed with vegetables and sometimes meat, then pan-fried until golden. This is the market's signature dish.
- **Mayak gimbap** – small, bite-sized seaweed rice rolls without meat, dipped in a mustard-soy sauce. The name literally translates to "drug gimbap," a nickname for how addictively easy they are to keep eating, not an actual ingredient.
- **Yukhoe** – Korean-style seasoned raw beef, often served with a raw egg yolk on top and thin slices of Asian pear. It's a specialty dish rather than an everyday snack, and pricier than most other market food.
- **Tteokbokki** – chewy rice cakes simmered in a spicy-sweet red sauce, a Korean street food staple you'll find at markets all over the country.
- **Sundae** – Korean blood sausage, usually made with noodles or glass noodles stuffed inside intestine casing, sliced and served with a salt-and-pepper dip.
- **Kalguksu** – hand-cut wheat noodles served in a hot broth, a heartier, less adventurous option if you want something familiar-tasting.

## How to Order and Eat Like a Local

Seating at most food stalls is communal: long shared benches or counters facing the cooking area, often with strangers sitting elbow to elbow. This is normal market culture, not a sign you're in the wrong place.

At most stalls, you order directly with the vendor, either by pointing at a photo, pointing at what the people next to you are eating, or naming the dish if you know it. Printed English menus are the exception rather than the rule here, so a little flexibility helps.

Cash is strongly preferred, and at many of the smaller open-air stalls it's effectively required, even if a card machine is visible. It's worth carrying a mix of 1,000 and 10,000 KRW bills before you go. Prices have risen in recent years along with ingredient costs, but as a rough guide, a solo visitor can eat well for well under 20,000 KRW, and a couple sharing a few dishes can expect somewhere in the 30,000–40,000 KRW range, more if you add yukhoe, which tends to be priced separately and by weight.

## Common Struggles for Foreigners (and How to Handle Them)

**Vendors calling out to you.** Stall owners actively calling out and gesturing for you to sit down is normal market behavior here, not a scam or a hard sell. You can politely wave it off if you're not ready, or just sit down if the food looks good.

**Overcharging, especially targeting foreign visitors.** This is worth taking seriously rather than brushing off. Gwangjang Market has had real, well-publicized overcharging incidents involving tourists, including a case that went viral after a vendor charged more than the posted menu price for sundae, and a more recent one involving a foreign visitor charged an inflated price for a bottle of water. It became enough of a problem that merchant groups have publicly disputed over it. None of this means you need to feel on edge the whole visit, but three habits will cover you:

- **Only order from stalls with a price list you can actually see.** If there's nothing posted, ask the price before you sit down, not after you've already started eating.
- **Confirm the total before you order**, especially for pricier items like yukhoe, and double-check if a vendor casually offers to add something extra ("want a bit of this too?") without stating the price first.
- **Carry cash.** Some stalls still don't reliably take cards even when a card reader is sitting right there.

**No visible menu, or a Korean-only one.** This is common, especially at smaller stalls. Looking at what's cooking, pointing, or checking what other customers ordered is a completely normal way to order here.

**Crowds and no personal space.** Between the shared seating and the narrow aisles, Gwangjang Market is a tight, high-contact experience by design. If that's not your thing, an early morning visit will feel noticeably calmer.

## Best Time to Visit

Hours vary quite a bit depending on which part of the market you're in. The general retail section (clothing, fabric, housewares) runs roughly 9 AM to 6 PM and is closed on Sundays. The food alley, the part most visitors actually come for, keeps a longer and different schedule: roughly 9 AM to 11 PM, open every day including Sundays. In other words, don't assume the whole market winds down in the evening, the food stalls are often just hitting their busiest stretch around dinnertime. Exact hours can vary stall by stall, so treat these as general guidance rather than a guarantee.

If you want a calmer visit, aim for around 9 to 11 AM, before the lunch crowd arrives. The lunch rush, roughly noon to 2 PM, brings a wave of office workers from nearby business districts, and things pick up again in the evening between about 6 and 8 PM. Weekends tend to be the most crowded overall. That said, Gwangjang Market has a reputation for being busy pretty much any time you go, so don't expect a truly empty market no matter when you show up.

## How to Get to Gwangjang Market by Subway

The market is served by **Jongno 5(o)-ga Station on Line 1**. **Exit 8** is generally considered the most direct route to the main food section, though several nearby exits (including 7, 9, and 10) also come out close to different sides of the market. **Euljiro 4-ga Station (Lines 2 and 5)** is also within walking distance, though the route involves crossing Cheonggyecheon and ends up being a longer walk than coming from Jongno 5-ga, so it's more of a backup option than a first choice. If you come up an exit and don't immediately see it, it's worth checking Naver Map or KakaoMap rather than Google Maps, since Google Maps' walking directions are unreliable in Korea.

## Nearby Combo Course: Dongdaemun, Cheonggyecheon, and Myeongdong

Gwangjang Market sits close to a few other spots that combine well into a longer day out.

**Dongdaemun**, Seoul's massive fashion and shopping district, is a short subway ride or walk away and makes sense if you want to follow up market food with some late-night shopping, since many Dongdaemun malls stay open unusually late.

**Cheonggyecheon**, the restored stream that runs through central Seoul, passes not far from the market and is a pleasant, walkable stretch if you want a break from the market's intensity.

## Quick Recap

Gwangjang Market is worth the crowds and the slightly chaotic first impression. Come hungry, bring cash, expect to share a bench with strangers, and don't be shy about pointing at food you can't name. Visit in the mid-morning if you want it calmer, or just accept that it's always going to be busy and dive in anyway.
