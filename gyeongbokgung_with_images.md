# Gyeongbokgung Palace & Hanbok: The One Combo Every First-Time Visitor Should Do

If you only visit one palace in Seoul, make it Gyeongbokgung. It's the biggest and most famous of the city's royal palaces, and it pairs perfectly with one of the most popular things to do in Seoul: renting a hanbok, Korea's traditional dress, and walking around the palace grounds in it.

This guide covers what Gyeongbokgung actually is, how the hanbok-for-free-entry trick works, where to rent one nearby, when to catch the Royal Guard Changing Ceremony, what to see inside, and the practical details (tickets, hours, subway) for a smooth first visit.

## What Is Gyeongbokgung Palace?

Gyeongbokgung was the first and largest royal palace of the Joseon Dynasty, originally built in 1395, just a few years after the dynasty itself was founded. For centuries it was the main seat of Korean royal power, the place where kings lived, ruled, and received foreign envoys.

Its history isn't all grand ceremony, though. The palace was almost completely burned down in 1592 during the Japanese invasions of Korea, and it sat in ruins for roughly 270 years afterward. It was finally rebuilt in the late 1800s under King Gojong, only to be deliberately dismantled again during the Japanese colonial period in the early 20th century, when most of its roughly 500 buildings were torn down or sold off. What you see today is the result of an ongoing restoration effort that's been running since the 1990s.

That history is part of why visiting feels different from just seeing "an old building." You're looking at a place that Korea has spent decades rebuilding after it was almost erased.

## The Hanbok Perk: Free Admission When You Dress Up

Here's the detail that makes Gyeongbokgung different from most palaces you'll visit anywhere else: if you're wearing a proper hanbok, you get in for free.

The catch is in what counts as "proper." According to the palace's own guidelines, you generally need a full top-and-bottom set, a jeogori (the jacket) paired with a skirt or pants, not just one hanbok piece worn with regular clothes. Mixing a hanbok jacket with jeans, or a hanbok skirt with a T-shirt, doesn't qualify. Both traditional and modern-style hanbok are accepted, but a plain one-piece "hanbok-style dress" without a proper jeogori generally doesn't count either.

If free entry matters to your plan, it's worth asking the rental shop directly whether the set you're choosing meets the palace's entry requirements, since staff deal with this question constantly and can point you to a compliant option.

## How to Rent a Hanbok Near the Palace

There are dozens of hanbok rental shops clustered in the streets around Gyeongbokgung, especially near the station exits on the side away from the palace entrance. Walk-ins are normal, most shops don't require booking ahead, and the process is quick: pick a style, get changed, and you're out the door.

Roughly speaking, expect a basic hanbok rental to run somewhere in the 15,000–25,000 KRW range for a few hours, with premium or designer-style hanbok costing more, sometimes 45,000 KRW or higher. Rental periods are usually offered in blocks (a few hours, a full day, or even overnight), and going over your time slot typically costs extra per hour. Fitting and changing time is generally not counted against your rental clock, so budget some extra time before you're actually "on the clock."

Exact prices, styles, and shop names change often, so treat any specific number here as a rough guide rather than a quote, and compare a couple of nearby shops before committing, prices can vary noticeably block to block.

## Royal Guard Changing Ceremony

Right in front of Gwanghwamun, the palace's main gate, you can catch the Royal Guard Changing Ceremony, a reenactment of the traditional guard rotation that once protected the palace, performed by staff in colorful historical uniforms.

The main ceremony runs twice a day, at 10:00 and 14:00, lasting about 20 minutes each time. There are also two shorter related events: the Gwanghwamun Gate Guard Ceremony at exactly 11:00 and 13:00, each lasting about 10 minutes, and a public Guard Training Demonstration at 09:30 and 13:30, each lasting about 15 minutes. All of this happens daily except Tuesdays, when the palace itself is closed.

Arrive a bit early if you want a good spot near the gate, this is one of the most photographed moments at the palace, and it draws a crowd fast.

## Inside the Palace: What to See

Once you're through the gate, a few buildings are worth prioritizing if your time is limited.

**Geunjeongjeon** is the main throne hall, the ceremonial heart of the palace, where kings once received officials and held state events. It's the largest and most imposing structure on the grounds, and usually the first thing that makes people stop and take photos.

**Gyeonghoeru Pavilion** is a large wooden pavilion built over a pond, originally constructed in the early 1400s and used for royal banquets and receptions for foreign envoys. It's especially photogenic in the late afternoon light or reflected in the water.

Beyond those two, the palace grounds are large enough that just wandering between courtyards, gates, and smaller halls is worthwhile on its own, especially if you're in hanbok and not in a hurry.

## Admission, Hours, and Closed Days

Regular admission is 3,000 KRW for adults. Discounted or free rates apply for children, teenagers, and seniors, but the exact age cutoffs vary depending on the source, so check the current rates on the official site rather than assuming a number. Admission is also free for everyone on "Culture Day," the last Wednesday of each month, on top of the hanbok exemption already mentioned.

Opening hours shift by season: roughly 9:00 AM to 5:00 PM in winter (November–February), 9:00 AM to 6:00 PM in spring and fall (March–May, September–October), and 9:00 AM to 6:30 PM in summer (June–August). Last admission is usually about an hour before closing.

The palace is closed on Tuesdays, though if a Tuesday happens to be a public holiday, it stays open that day and closes on the next non-holiday day instead.

Because ticket prices, hours, and occasional special night-viewing events change from year to year, it's worth double-checking current details on the official Royal Palaces and Tombs Center site (royal.khs.go.kr) before you go.

## How to Get to Gyeongbokgung by Subway

The palace has its own dedicated station: **Gyeongbokgung Station on Line 3**. Exit 5 leads directly toward the palace entrance and Gwanghwamun.

If you're planning to rent a hanbok first, most rental shops are clustered closer to Exits 2, 3, and 4, on the side streets around Sajik-ro, rather than right at Exit 5. It's a short walk either way, so a common route is to come up at Exit 4 or so, get changed into a hanbok nearby, then walk over to the palace entrance from there.

## Best Time to Visit

Mornings, especially right at opening, tend to be the quietest time to explore before tour groups and midday crowds build up. If catching the Royal Guard Changing Ceremony is a priority, plan around the 10:00 or 14:00 showings and get there a little early.

Spring (cherry blossoms) and fall (autumn foliage) are the most popular seasons to visit, partly because the palace grounds photograph beautifully then, and partly because the weather makes wearing a hanbok for a few hours far more comfortable than the height of summer or the middle of winter.

## Nearby Combo Course: Bukchon, Gwanghwamun, Insadong

Gyeongbokgung sits at the center of a cluster of neighborhoods that combine easily into a half-day or full-day route.

**Bukchon Hanok Village**, a hillside neighborhood of traditional Korean houses, is a short walk or taxi ride away and makes for a natural next stop, especially if you're already in a hanbok and want more traditional-looking backdrops. **Gwanghwamun Square**, right at the palace's front gate, has its own statues and open plaza worth a quick look even outside guard ceremony times. **Insadong**, known for traditional crafts, tea houses, and art galleries, is also close enough to fold into the same day.

A simple route: hanbok rental and Gyeongbokgung in the morning, Bukchon in the early afternoon while the light is still good for photos, then Insadong or Gwanghwamun Square to wind down before returning your hanbok.

## Quick Recap

Gyeongbokgung is the palace to prioritize in Seoul, and pairing it with a hanbok rental is less a gimmick and more the intended experience, it even gets you free admission if the outfit qualifies. Time your visit around the Royal Guard Changing Ceremony, prioritize Geunjeongjeon and Gyeonghoeru Pavilion if you're short on time, and use the extra energy to walk over to Bukchon or Insadong afterward.

*Admission fees, hours, ceremony times, and hanbok rental prices can change. Check the official Royal Palaces and Tombs Center site (royal.khs.go.kr) and Visit Seoul (english.visitseoul.net) for the latest details before you go.*
