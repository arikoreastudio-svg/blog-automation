# Essential Apps & Prep for Your Korea Trip

A surprising amount of first-trip frustration in Korea comes down to a handful of small things you can sort out before you even board the plane: the right map app, a way to get online the moment you land, and a plan for cash versus card. None of this is complicated, but figuring it out after you've already landed, jet-lagged and hauling luggage, is a lot more annoying than doing it in five minutes at home.

This guide covers the apps and prep that actually matter for a first Korea trip: maps, translation, getting around, staying connected, and paying for things, plus a short checklist you can run through before you leave.

## Maps: Skip Google Maps

Start here, because this is the one that catches almost everyone off guard. **Google Maps does not give reliable walking or transit directions in Korea.** Due to long-standing local mapping restrictions, it often can't route you on foot or show live subway and bus info, and a lot of visitors don't find this out until they're already standing on a corner, confused.

Download **Naver Map** or **KakaoMap** instead. Both have English interfaces, accurate walking directions, live transit routing, and correct subway exit numbers, the exit number detail matters more than it sounds like at busy stations with a dozen exits. Our [Seoul subway guide](https://korea-travel-guide-kr.blogspot.com/2026/07/seoul-subway-guide.html) goes into more detail on why this matters and how to actually use these apps for transit, but the short version is: install one of them before you land, not after.

## Translation: Papago

For text and conversation, **Papago**, made by Naver (the same company behind Naver Map), is generally considered the strongest translation app for Korean specifically, often noticeably better than general-purpose translation apps at handling Korean sentence structure and levels of politeness.

It's useful in a few different ways: point your camera at a menu or sign for an instant overlay translation, type or paste text, or use conversation mode to go back and forth with someone in person, you speak into the app, it plays a Korean translation out loud, and vice versa. You can also download offline language packs in advance, worth doing before you land in case you don't have data right away.

## Getting Around: Kakao T for Taxis

For taxis, **Kakao T** is the app almost everyone in Korea uses to call a ride, similar in concept to Uber or Grab, and it works with licensed, regulated taxis rather than private drivers. You can set the app to English, and because you type your destination instead of saying it out loud, it sidesteps a lot of the language barrier that comes with flagging down a cab and explaining where you're going.

One thing worth knowing before you land: registering a foreign credit card for automatic in-app payment isn't always straightforward, since the standard sign-up flow is built around a Korean phone number. The simplest workaround is to select "pay the driver directly" when booking, which lets you pay in cash or by card at the end of the ride the normal way. Ride-hailing apps built specifically for international visitors, designed to accept foreign cards directly, are also starting to appear, so it's worth checking what's currently available when you're planning your trip.

For everyday transit (subway and bus), you'll want a T-money card rather than Kakao T, our [Seoul subway guide](https://korea-travel-guide-kr.blogspot.com/2026/07/seoul-subway-guide.html) covers how to get one and how fares and transfers work.

## Staying Connected: SIM, eSIM, or Pocket WiFi

You've got three realistic options for internet in Korea, and the right one depends mostly on your phone and how many people you're traveling with.

**eSIM** is generally the easiest option if your phone supports it: you can buy and activate a plan before you even leave home, so you're online the moment you land, no counter visit required. **A physical SIM card** works well too and is the safer bet if your phone is older or you're not sure it supports eSIM, you'll get a Korean phone number along with data. **Pocket WiFi** (a small rentable router) makes the most sense for groups or families, since one device can connect several phones at once, though it does mean carrying and charging an extra gadget.

All three are sold at counters in Incheon Airport's arrival hall, generally open long hours if not 24/7, with English-speaking staff at the major carrier counters. It's usually cheaper to reserve online in advance (through a site like Klook or KKday) and simply pick up at the airport counter than to buy on the spot, and data plans and prices change often enough that it's worth comparing current options shortly before your trip rather than relying on last year's numbers. If you're coming straight from Incheon, our [airport transportation guide](https://korea-travel-guide-kr.blogspot.com/2026/07/incheon-airport-to-seoul.html) covers the rest of that first hour, getting from the terminal into the city.

## Money: Cards Work, But Bring Some Cash

Korea is a genuinely card-friendly country, Visa and Mastercard are accepted almost everywhere, even for small purchases. That said, don't assume you'll never need cash: a real share of Korean stores, especially smaller ones, still don't have contactless (tap-to-pay) terminals even when they take chip cards, and traditional markets, some street food stalls, and small older restaurants are often cash-only or cash-preferred.

A reasonable approach is to withdraw or exchange some cash on arrival, mostly in smaller bills, enough to comfortably cover a market visit or a street food afternoon, and rely on your card for everything else. Airport currency exchange counters are convenient but typically don't offer the best rate; if getting a better exchange rate matters to you, city currency exchange shops (Myeongdong has a well-known cluster of them) tend to be more competitive, though this takes a bit more effort than exchanging at the airport. All-in-one prepaid cards that combine currency exchange, everyday spending, and a T-money transit chip have also become popular with tourists in recent years, worth looking into if you'd rather not juggle multiple cards.

One specific option worth knowing about is **WOWPASS**, a prepaid card you can pick up right at kiosks in Incheon Airport: you load it with a foreign credit or debit card, use it like a regular prepaid card for everyday spending, and it also carries a T-money transit function that you can top up from your card balance through the app, so it doubles as a way to handle currency exchange and transit in one card rather than two. The exact terms, fees, and kiosk locations can change, so it's worth checking the current details before you land rather than assuming last year's setup still applies.

One more thing worth knowing: **Korea doesn't have a tipping culture.** Restaurants, taxis, and hotels don't expect tips, and offering one can sometimes come across as awkward rather than generous.

## Other Apps: A Realistic Note

Korea has excellent food delivery and reservation apps, but a lot of them are genuinely difficult for short-term foreign visitors to use, many require a Korean phone number, a Korean bank card, or even local ID verification to sign up. Don't be surprised or discouraged if an app a local friend recommends turns out to be hard to set up in the few days you're in town. For most trip-planning and day-to-day navigation purposes, the apps covered above (maps, translation, taxis, and connectivity) will cover the vast majority of what you actually need.

## Pre-Departure Checklist

- **Naver Map or KakaoMap** downloaded (not just Google Maps)
- **Papago** downloaded, with an offline Korean language pack if you want a backup
- **Kakao T** installed and set to English, so it's ready to go for your first taxi
- **eSIM, physical SIM, or pocket WiFi** decided on and, ideally, reserved in advance
- Some **Korean won in cash** planned for on arrival, plus a card that works internationally
- A **T-money card** on your list for once you land, covered in our [Seoul subway guide](https://korea-travel-guide-kr.blogspot.com/2026/07/seoul-subway-guide.html)

## Quick Recap

None of this takes long to sort out, but doing it before you land makes the first day of your trip noticeably smoother: real map directions from the start, a way to communicate, a taxi app that actually works for you, internet the moment you land, and a sensible mix of cash and card.
