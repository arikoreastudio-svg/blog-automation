# Seoul Subway Guide: Everything a First-Time Visitor Needs to Know

Seoul's subway is one of the easiest big-city transit systems in the world to figure out, even if you don't speak Korean and this is your first day in the country. It's clean, it runs constantly, and almost everything, signs, announcements, ticket machines, is in English as well as Korean.

This guide covers how the system works, how to get and use a transit card, what things actually cost, how transfers work, which apps to trust (and which one to avoid), operating hours, a few manners worth knowing, and the mistakes first-timers make most often.

If you're arriving from the airport, our [guide to getting from Incheon Airport into Seoul](https://korea-travel-guide-kr.blogspot.com/2026/07/incheon-airport-to-seoul.html) covers that leg of the trip. This guide picks up once you're actually in the city and riding the subway day to day.

## What Kind of System Is This?

The Seoul subway (technically the Seoul Metropolitan Subway) is a large network, nine main numbered lines at its core, plus several additional lines that extend out into the wider metropolitan area, adding up to more than 20 lines and several hundred stations in total. As a visitor, you'll mostly stick to the numbered lines: Line 1 is dark blue, Line 2 (the big loop line through central Seoul) is green, Line 3 is orange, Line 4 is light blue, and so on, each line has its own color, and that color shows up on train exteriors, platform signs, and every map.

Every station also has a three-digit code (the first digit is the line number), so even if you can't read the Korean station name, you can match numbers. Signage is in Korean, English, and usually Chinese and Japanese too, and train announcements are made in Korean and English at minimum. This is genuinely one of the more foreigner-friendly subway systems anywhere, so don't over-prepare, it's very learnable in your first day or two.

## Getting a Transit Card (T-money)

The easiest way to pay is a **T-money card**, a rechargeable transit card that works on the subway, city buses, and even some taxis. You don't need to plan ahead: T-money cards are sold at any convenience store (GS25, CU, 7-Eleven, and similar) near a subway station, as well as at ticket machines inside the stations themselves.

A blank card generally costs somewhere around 2,500–4,000 KRW (prices vary a bit by design), and that's just for the card, you then load it with however much travel credit you want, in cash, at the same convenience store counter or at a station machine. Reloading is simple and can be done again any time your balance runs low.

If you'd rather not bother with a card, subway stations also sell **single-journey paper tickets** from machines: you pay the exact fare plus a refundable 500 KRW deposit, and get the deposit back by inserting the used ticket into a separate "deposit refund" machine near the exit gates. It works, but it's more steps than just tapping a T-money card, so most visitors staying more than a day or two find the card more convenient.

## How Much Does It Cost?

As of the most recent fare adjustment, the base subway fare with a T-money card is roughly **1,550 KRW**, covering your first 10 km, with a small additional charge (around 100 KRW) kicking in every few kilometers after that. Paying with a single-journey paper ticket instead of a card usually costs about 100 KRW more per ride.

If you're up early, there's also an **early-bird discount**: tap in with a transit card on your first ride of the day before 6:30 AM and the base fare drops by about 20%, to roughly 1,240 KRW, a nice bonus if your trip has you heading to the airport or an early sunrise spot before the city wakes up.

Fares in Seoul do change from time to time, so treat these numbers as a solid ballpark rather than gospel, the subway apps mentioned below will always show you the exact current fare for your specific trip before you tap in.

## Tap In, Tap Out, and How Transfers Work

With a T-money card, you tap the card on the reader both when you enter through the gate and again when you exit, this is what lets the system calculate distance-based fares correctly, so don't skip the tap-out even though it can feel unnecessary.

One of the best things about T-money is that **transfers between subway and bus are free or heavily discounted**, as long as you transfer within a set time window (about 30 minutes generally, extended to around 60 minutes late at night) and use the same card the whole way. Instead of paying a fresh base fare for the bus, the system just adds the extra distance to what you already paid. This makes combining subway and bus for a single trip much cheaper than paying for each separately, so it's worth getting comfortable mixing the two.

## The Apps You Actually Need (Skip Google Maps)

This is the single most common mistake foreign visitors make: **Google Maps does not reliably give walking or transit directions in Korea.** Due to long-standing mapping data restrictions, Google Maps in Korea often can't route you properly on foot or show live subway/bus info, and travelers who don't know this end up wandering around confused on day one.

Use one of these instead:

- **Naver Map** or **KakaoMap** – Korea's own map apps, both available in English, with accurate walking directions, live transit routing, and real subway exit numbers.
- **Kakao Subway** or **Subway Korea** (or similar dedicated subway apps) – handy for a clean line map, real-time train arrivals, and transfer directions without the extra clutter of a full map app.

Download at least one of these before you land, or as soon as you're on Wi-Fi. It will save you a genuinely large amount of frustration.

## First Trains, Last Trains, and Missing the Last One

Seoul's subway generally starts running around **5:30 AM** (a bit later, about 30 minutes, on Sundays and public holidays) and the last trains depart around **midnight**, though exact first/last train times vary by line and by station, so check your specific station in one of the apps above rather than assuming.

If you do miss the last train, you're not stuck: Seoul runs a network of late-night "**Owl Buses**" (routes starting with N), running through the overnight hours on set routes across the city, and you can pay with the same T-money card. Otherwise, taxis are widely available around the city all night, just note that late-night taxi fares run higher than daytime rates.

## A Few Manners Worth Knowing

- **Priority seats** near the ends of each car are reserved for elderly passengers, pregnant women, and people with disabilities, it's generally expected that other riders leave these open even if the train looks empty elsewhere.
- **Pink seats**, specifically marked for pregnant passengers, are treated as off-limits to everyone else, even if unoccupied.
- Phone calls are generally kept short and quiet on board, and many riders default to texting instead of talking.
- Eating on the subway isn't strictly banned, but strong-smelling food is generally frowned upon in the enclosed car.

None of this is rigidly enforced, but noticing and following these norms goes a long way toward blending in rather than standing out.

## Common Mistakes First-Timers Make

**Exit number confusion.** Big stations can have ten, fifteen, or more numbered exits, and picking the wrong one can add a genuinely long walk to your trip, especially at major interchange stations. Before you head up the stairs, check which exit number your destination is closest to in Naver Map or KakaoMap rather than guessing.

**Assuming "same station" means "same spot."** At some interchange stations, especially older ones where two separate lines were built years apart, the platforms for different lines can be a long underground walk from each other, or the station might even go by two adjacent station names depending on which line you're on. If your route involves a transfer, give yourself a few extra minutes rather than assuming it's a quick hop.

**Not checking the direction before boarding.** Trains on the same line run in two directions, so double-check the destination shown on the platform screen or train headboard before getting on, it's an easy mistake to make when you're jet-lagged on day one.

## Putting It Into Practice

Once you've got a T-money card in your pocket and Naver Map or KakaoMap installed, the Seoul subway becomes genuinely easy to use, arguably easier than driving or figuring out buses in a lot of other major cities. It's how most visitors get around for shopping trips to neighborhoods like [Myeongdong](https://korea-travel-guide-kr.blogspot.com/2026/07/myeongdong-travel-guide.html), day trips to palaces, or just exploring different neighborhoods without needing a car or constant taxis.

## Quick Recap

Get a T-money card at any convenience store near a station, tap in and out every ride, use Naver Map or KakaoMap instead of Google Maps, and keep an eye on the clock if you're out past midnight. Everything else, the colors, the numbers, the announcements, is built to be figured out on the fly.
